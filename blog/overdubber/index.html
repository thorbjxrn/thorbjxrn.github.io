From tape machines to Overdubber: building the recorder I wanted

By Thorbjørn Bonvik · 18 October 2026

Overdubber is a looper and multitrack recorder for iPhone and iPad. Record a part, listen back while you add another, and keep building. A melody becomes a harmony. A beat becomes something you can play along with.

I made the first prototype in 2019, for a project at Swinburne University of Technology in Melbourne, Australia. Seven years later, I picked the idea up again.

The idea in Melbourne

Looping and stacking were at the centre of it from the beginning. I sketched out the first vision on paper at the university campus, back when SoundCloud was where I imagined people sharing what they made.

I had a great time at Swinburne. It was where I became interested in iOS apps and UX design. After Melbourne, I kept reading, and Dieter Rams became a big inspiration for this app in particular.

For a while, though, music production got most of my attention outside work. My job took most of my programming energy. Spending that time making music helped me understand more clearly what I wanted from a recording app.

Two tape machines

Along the way, I got interested in analogue tape. My first machine was a Grundig TK 248, bought in “condition unknown.” I had a lot of fun with it.

But lo-fi. Very, very lo-fi.

Later, I bought a Tandberg TD-20A. It is an amazing piece of engineering, and it became a big inspiration for the look and feel of Overdubber.

Picking it up again

By mid-2026, the concept was clearer than ever in my mind. I still hadn’t found a recorder that worked the way I wanted, so I came back to the prototype.

The sharing part had changed. I was now thinking about TikTok and Reels: showing the performance, and letting people watch the layers come together.

That led to Session mode. It records the app with a picture-in-picture view of your performance, so you can share the process of building a loop. It is now available in Overdubber.

I’ve also started posting on Overdubber’s TikTok. That feels like a fitting place for an idea that was always about making something and sharing it.

Try it

You can use Overdubber to stack vocal harmonies, record guitar over a melody, or build a beat one part at a time. The free app lets you record up to eight layers, balance them, and export a WAV or M4A mix. A one-time purchase adds unlimited layers, audio import, individual-track export, more themes, and removes ads.

And that’s pretty much the story. A university project, a few years of making music, and two tape machines. Don’t let your ideas stay ideas, I guess.
